    How these are calculated

    Ranges from real runners. Blythe & Király ("Prediction and quantification of individual athletic performance of runners", PLOS ONE 2016) published the thepowerof10 results database: official times for 165,000 British club runners from 1500 m to the marathon. For every pair of distances, scripts/race_errors.py takes each runner's two races closest in time (within 90 days) and compares the second time with VDOT's prediction from the first (data/ref/race_errors.csv). At the same distance it's two different races, so a race predicts its own distance with the spread of ordinary race-to-race variation. The prediction is VDOT shifted by the median miss, and the range is the 10th to 90th percentile, separately for VDOT under 40, 40–50, 50–60 and over 60 (the input race's VDOT at sea level), since slower runners are less predictable. For example, at VDOT 40–50 a 10K predicts a 5K 1.2% faster than VDOT, with a range of 6.2% faster to 3.0% slower. A half predicts a marathon 9.3% slower. Distances in between are interpolated on log distance. Checks: built from half the runners, 71–83% of the other half land inside (80% for most pairs). In Vickers & Vertosick's survey of 2,303 recreational runners (self-reported times, races up to six months apart), 76% land inside. Survey runners vary more between short races (5K → 10K: 65%) and slightly less in the marathon (83–86%).

    VDOT (Daniels & Gilbert, Oxygen Power, 1979) is the same formula as the Fitness tab. Riegel's formula (time × distance ratio1.06) misses by about the same amounts, so it isn't shown separately.

    Marathon with weekly mileage uses Vickers & Vertosick's models ("An empirical study of race times in recreational endurance runners", BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil 2016;8:26), the ones FiveThirtyEight's marathon calculator is built on. Model 1, one race: marathon speed (m/s) = 0.1602 + 0.8308 × Riegel speed (exponent 1.07, from your longest ticked race) + 0.0642 × weekly miles ÷ 10. Model 2, two races: the exponent between your two longest ticked races, k, gives the marathon exponent 1.4511 − 0.2380 × k − 0.0141 × weekly miles ÷ 10. Their ranges come from the paper's validation runners: 80% within −6.4% to +7.8% (model 1, n = 310) and −6.2% to +7.3% (model 2, n = 156). When mileage is entered, they replace the races' VDOT estimate for the marathon.

    LT2 is treated as a race at that pace. HRV4Training (Marco Altini, "Lactate threshold / critical speed") defines threshold, or critical pace, as what you could hold all out for 10–15 km. That's a 12.5 km race, with the range widened to cover 10 to 15 km. Daniels' T pace is about the pace you could race for an hour, so it's a 60-minute race. HRV4Training's zones are percentages of that pace (Z1 slower than 130%, Z2 115–130%, Z3 105–115%, Z4 95–105%, Z5 90–95%, Z6 faster than 90%). Without an LT2 they're based on the predicted 12.5 km pace.

    LT1 is taken as the top of Daniels' Easy range: 70% of VDOT (the Fitness tab's Easy pace) to 74% (Daniels' Running Formula: easy running is 59–74% of VO2max). The VDOT those give is predicted forward like a race. No study has checked LT1 pace against race times, so its range is that 70–74% span combined with the spread of a 10K-based prediction. That's a floor, not a measured range, so LT1 feeds the prediction only when there's no race or LT2; otherwise it's listed for comparison.

    Pooling: inputs are averaged on log time, each weighted by 1 ÷ its range². The pooled range is the weighted average of the inputs' ranges, not narrower. In the club data, pooling a 5K and a 10K to predict a half cut the typical error only from 5.3% to 5.0%, because most of the spread is the runner's own make-up (endurance vs. speed), which repeats across their races. Training paces use the VDOT of the pooled focus-distance prediction.

    Altitude: each input is converted to the prediction elevation two published ways before predicting. Daniels: paces slow 4–5 s/mi per 1,000 ft above 3,000 ft. NCAA 2009 altitude tables: % added by venue altitude for the mile, 5K and 10K. The prediction is the average of the methods, and the range covers all of them. LT1 and LT2 are converted as a 10K would be. The reference runners raced in Britain, near sea level.

    Altini & Amft's EMBC 2018 paper estimates 10K time from HRV, training volume and intensity distribution by regression, but doesn't publish the coefficients, so it isn't used here.
