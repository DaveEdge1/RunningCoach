How these are calculated

Load is HR-zone-weighted minutes: each minute in Z1 counts 1, Z2 counts 2, and so on up to Z5 = 5. Runs without real HR count 2.5 per minute.

Long-term / recent load are exponentially weighted averages of daily load over 42 and 7 days (often called fitness and fatigue, CTL and ATL). They measure how much you train, not how fit you are: steady training keeps them flat even while you improve. Fitness gains show up in pace at AeT and VDOT. History begins Aug 19, 2026, so both start at the average load of the first four weeks.

Form measures training fatigue only. Sleep, stress, heat, and illness aren't included, so read single runs with that in mind.

Pace at AeT uses only seconds with HR 138–152 bpm, moving faster than walking, on grades under 2%, scaled to exactly 145 bpm. A run needs at least 4 minutes of those seconds. The trend weights each run by how many seconds qualified over the past 14 days, and each point is drawn at the center of its window, so the line stops about 7 days short of today. Only runs within 1,000 ft of Flagstaff (7,000 ft) count, so the number is a Flagstaff-altitude pace.

Zones come from config.json: LT1 (AeT) 145 bpm and LT2 (LT) 168 bpm. The intensity chart puts wider breaks at those two thresholds.

VDOT is Jack Daniels' formula applied to your most recent race (races.csv, or a planned race) or time trial (a planned session named "time trial"). The next-race estimate uses that current fitness with no projected gains. Elevation comes from the run's own file.

Altitude is converted two published ways and shown as a range. Daniels: paces slow 4–5 s/mi per 1,000 ft above 3,000 ft (15–18 s/mi at 7,000 ft). NCAA 2009 altitude tables: % added by venue altitude (0–7,703 ft) for the mile, 5K and 10K, interpolated by altitude and distance, and held at the nearest of those distances outside that span. Neither accounts for acclimatization.
