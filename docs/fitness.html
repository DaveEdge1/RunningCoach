What these numbers mean

Long-term / recent load are exponentially weighted averages of daily load over 42 and 7 days (often called fitness and fatigue, CTL and ATL). They measure how much you train, not how fit you are: steady training keeps them flat even while you improve. Fitness gains show up in pace at AeT and VDOT (Fitness tab). History begins Aug 19, 2026, so both start at the average load of the first four weeks.

Form is long-term minus recent load, as a % of long-term load going into the day: above +5% reads fresh, below −10% carrying fatigue. It measures training fatigue only. Sleep, stress, heat, and illness aren't included, so read single runs with that in mind.

Load ramp is the acute:chronic workload ratio: load over the last 7 days divided by the weekly average over the last 28. Gabbett (2016, Br J Sports Med 50:273) calls 0.8–1.3 the sweet spot and ≥ 1.5 the danger zone for injury. The ratio has been criticized as a predictor (Impellizzeri et al. 2020, Int J Sports Physiol Perform 15:907), so read it as a guide to how fast load is rising, not a risk score. It needs four weeks of history.

Pace at AeT uses grade-adjusted pace (Minetti et al. 2002) from seconds with HR 138–152 bpm, moving faster than walking, on grades under 5%, scaled to exactly 145 bpm. It skips the first 10 minutes (warm-up), everything after 90 minutes (cardiac drift), and everything after a hard bout, when HR stays high for the rest of the run. A hard bout is 4 minutes at 155 bpm or 2 at 165, on an exponential curve through those points (shorter the higher the HR), counted after the warm-up. Runs within one day per 3 km of a race (Daniels' easy days after racing: 3 days for a 10K) show as open circles and stay out of the trend. Trail runs (marked in notes.md) are left out: grade adjustment doesn't account for rough footing. A run needs at least 4 minutes of those seconds. The trend is the median of the runs in the past 28 days (at least 3 runs), so one odd day doesn't move it, and each point is drawn at the center of its window, so the line stops about 14 days short of today. Only runs within 1,000 ft of Flagstaff (7,000 ft) count, so the number is a Flagstaff-altitude pace. Dots are colored by form going into that day, so a slow dot on a tired day isn't a sign of lost fitness.

VDOT is Jack Daniels' formula applied to your most recent race (races.csv, or a planned race) or time trial (a planned session named "time trial"). Training efforts never replace it: they are run below race effort by design, and a fastest window can include recoveries. The next-race estimate uses that current fitness with no projected gains. Elevation comes from the run's own file.

How it's calculated

The sections are fixed GPS start and end points in config.json (benchmarks): about 1.16 mi of the upper Schultz trail, timed in each direction separately, so a run can include either one. A pass counts from the start point to the next arrival at the end point, if its distance is within 10% of the section's (no detours). Average HR leaves out the first 60 s, while HR catches up with the change of grade.

At 145 bpm scales each time to the same effort, your AeT: on a fixed grade the energy cost per metre is constant (Minetti et al. 2002), so the oxygen used above rest rises in proportion to speed, and the share of oxygen reserve tracks the share of HR reserve (Swain & Leutholtz 1997). So time × (HR − 50) ÷ (145 − 50): a pass 5 bpm above AeT is credited about 5%. It works best on passes run steadily near AeT; it reads slow when you're tired (the day after a workout), hot, or on snow or mud, and fast when HR reads low (the arm strap). Same trail each time, so footing and grade cancel out, which grade-adjusted pace can't do for a trail.

How it's calculated

Sea-level VDOT over time. The dotted line is steady progress from current fitness to the goal on race day, the same path the checkpoint uses. Races and time trials are measured results. Between them, each small dot is an HR-based estimate from one run's steady laps at or near AeT (pace and HR together, so it needs no all-out effort; reps and races are left out, since HR lags pace above AeT). Estimates are scaled (× 1.025) so the trend line passes through 5K time trial (Nov 3). The dashed line is the trend over the last 56 days: a Theil–Sen line (the median of pairwise slopes, Sen 1968, so one odd run can't tip it) through the estimates; its rate is in the legend.

HR-based estimate estimates fitness from pace and HR together, so any steady easy running counts. Each steady lap gives an effective VO2max: the oxygen cost of its grade-adjusted pace (Daniels' equation, the one inside VDOT) divided by the share of VO2 reserve it used, which Swain & Leutholtz (1997, Med Sci Sports Exerc 29:410) found equal to the share of HR reserve (resting 50, max 188 bpm, from config.json). A steady lap leaves out its first 2 minutes (HR still catching up with pace), the run's first 10 minutes, everything after 90 minutes (cardiac drift), grades over 5%, and laps after a hard bout that aren't hard themselves (HR stays raised on jogs and cool-downs). It needs 4 minutes left and grade-adjusted speed that varies less than 10% (no strides or surges). Trail runs are left out, and so is any lap averaging over 148 bpm (AeT 145 plus a little sensor noise): above AeT HR and VO2 keep rising for 10–20 minutes (the slow component; Poole & Jones 2012, Compr Physiol 2:933), so a rep's average HR understates its cost and reads high, while a race's drifts up and reads low. A population formula doesn't fit one runner exactly, so every estimate is scaled by one factor, chosen so the trend line (last 56 days) passes through your latest race or time trial: the most direct measure of current fitness (Runalyze likewise scales its effective VO2max to race results; a race marked trail in notes.md doesn't count, since rough footing makes it read slow). Estimates are converted to sea level like a race. Single runs scatter by a point or two (sleep, fatigue, a few beats of sensor error), so read the trend, not one dot.

How it's calculated

The fastest grade-adjusted time over each distance inside any run, in the last 28 days and in all data ("Both" when one effort is best in both). Grade adjustment (Minetti) turns each stretch into its flat-ground equivalent, so a downhill stretch is slowed and a hilly one credited; the VDOT is on that time, at sea level, so efforts at different elevations compare fairly. A training window can hold recoveries (a whole interval session inside a 5K window) and is run below race effort by design, so it is a record, not race fitness: only races and time trials set current fitness. How it's calculated

Altitude is converted two published ways and shown as a range. Daniels: paces slow 4–5 s/mi per 1,000 ft above 3,000 ft (15–18 s/mi at 7,000 ft).
